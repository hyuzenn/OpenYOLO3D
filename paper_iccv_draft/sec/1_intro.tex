\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Streaming 3D perception commits online: a map or a planner's object memory
consumes each frame as it
arrives~\cite{streamingperception,streaming3d,conceptfusion,conceptgraphs}.
Per-frame emission makes that commitment
cheap and unstable --- a re-detected object arrives as a new identity, an
open-vocabulary label changes class between frames --- and per-frame detection
metrics neither see nor penalise it. Temporal confirmation is the natural
correction, and an old one~\cite{blackmanpopoli,sort,deepsort,ab3dmot}: report
a track only once it has been observed $N$ times, so transient errors never
reach the consumer. But a rule that reports later necessarily reports less; it
either delays commitment or withholds what it is not yet sure of, and both are
paid on the streaming path.

Whether that buys anything is harder to establish than it looks. Confirmation
does not merely reorder a stream --- it changes how many detections enter it
and how many identities the system carries --- and detection- and
association-oriented metrics are strongly coupled to both. A confirmed stream
compared against an immediately emitted or score-thresholded one therefore
differs in two things at once: a method emitting fewer instances, or
maintaining fewer identities, can post a better association score without
associating better. The benefit has to be measured at a
\emph{matched budget}, against controls granted the same number of emitted
detections and, separately, the same number of emitted identities. We study temporal
confirmation as a streaming emission policy, and ask first whether its
consistency benefit survives that comparison, then whether the benefit can be
retained without delaying the stream.

It survives. On nuScenes with a frozen CenterPoint detector, confirmation improves
association consistency under both matchings, in both association frames, and
against confidence accumulation~\cite{cbmot} as well as a static threshold, so
the gain is not an artifact of carrying fewer identities. At the identical
detection budget the same stream loses detection recall, DetA and aggregate
HOTA: the gain is bought rather than added. Applied unchanged to an indoor
open-vocabulary system sharing no detector or modality, the same rule reduces
class-label switching by $26.9\%$ at a matched identity count.

Both costs descend from one decision, that an observation is emitted only
once its track is confirmed. Releasing the withheld observations after the
fact delays them; discarding them instead reverses the association gain
outright in the world frame. We therefore split
the two decisions that rule fuses: \emph{when an output is emitted} and
\emph{when its status is confirmed}. Every observation is emitted at the frame
it occurs, marked provisional; confirmation later changes only its status, a
shift in its track's class vote relabels it, and a track that never
confirms is retracted within a bounded window. Confirmation leaves the critical streaming
path: the policy retains $160{,}938$ observations ($+43.6\%$) that
prefix-deleting causal emission discards, at zero emission latency, and revision
raises AMOTA by $0.0823$. It is not free either: against that arm it gives up
class-agnostic detection and identity quality, and a consumer that must hold
each object the moment it comes close trades most of that arm's misses for a
far larger false-alert load (Sec.~\ref{sec:consumer}).

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Streaming output policy.} We formulate streaming 3D perception
as a provisional-output decision problem and develop a training-free policy
that emits every observation at the frame it occurs and retrospectively
confirms, relabels or retracts it as evidence accumulates, without
emission-time latency; a bounded retraction horizon reproduces the unbounded
retrospective output exactly.
\item \textbf{Temporal-selection finding.} We show that temporal confirmation
yields gains in outdoor identity consistency and indoor semantic consistency,
and that these gains are not explained simply by reducing output volume.
\item \textbf{Matched-budget evaluation.} We evaluate temporal confirmation
under separately matched detection and identity budgets, and against a
confidence-accumulation lifecycle, distinguishing temporal selection from
changes in output volume and identity count.
\end{itemize}


\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\noindent\textbf{Streaming perception.} Streaming perception scores
predictions against the world at the moment they are \emph{emitted}, folding
latency into streaming AP (sAP)~\cite{streamingperception}; later work
optimises detectors for this regime~\cite{streamyolo} and processes LiDAR
sectors as they arrive rather than waiting for a full sweep~\cite{streaming3d}.
sAP remains a per-frame average precision, so two systems emitting identical
boxes at identical latency but differing entirely in identity continuity and
label stability receive the same score.

\noindent\textbf{Track lifecycle and confirmation.} The classical radar
literature decides when a candidate track is real enough to report with
\emph{track confirmation} --- under $M$-out-of-$N$ history logic a track is
confirmed once it has been assigned a detection in $M$ of the last $N$ updates
--- and designs confirmation, deletion, and track management
together~\cite{blackmanpopoli}; the same literature established that reporting
may lag estimation, \emph{retrodiction} revising earlier estimates once later
measurements arrive~\cite{retrodiction}. Our distinction is in the output
semantics: we revise records already emitted to the streaming consumer, while
retaining zero emission latency and allowing unconfirmed records to be
retracted. Tracking-by-detection inherited the
count-based half: SORT~\cite{sort} and DeepSORT~\cite{deepsort} withhold a
tentative track until matched in $N$ consecutive frames and
AB3DMOT~\cite{ab3dmot} carries the same minimum-hits rule onto 3D boxes, while
CenterPoint~\cite{centerpoint} reports tracks from their first observation. We
use this confirmation test at its conventional operating point rather than
proposing a new one.

\noindent\textbf{Confidence in the track lifecycle.} Recent 3D tracking has
largely moved this decision from counting to confidence: CBMOT~\cite{cbmot}
replaces the minimum-hits criterion with a propagated tracklet score fused with
each matched detection score, SimpleTrack~\cite{simpletrack} names
\emph{output} as a lifecycle decision separate from birth and death, and
Poly-MOT~\cite{polymot} and Fast-Poly~\cite{fastpoly} pair category-specific
association with mixed count- and confidence-based termination. We take this
line as a comparator rather than a competitor: we measure what temporal
selection changes at a fixed associator, not absolute tracking scores.
Both rules decide which detections reach the output and both emit less, which
is why we compare them at matched output and identity budgets.

\noindent\textbf{Open-vocabulary 3D perception.} This setting has moved from
offline labelling of a completed
reconstruction~\cite{openscene,lerf,openmask3d,openyolo3d} to incremental
object maps~\cite{conceptfusion,conceptgraphs} and to fully online instance
segmentation that maintains persistent identities across
frames~\cite{embodiedsam,onlineanyseg,autoseg3d}. Cross-frame identity is thus
an acknowledged design goal, but this work is scored by per-scene
instance-segmentation AP, so how often a persistent instance's \emph{class
label} changes as observations accumulate is never quantified.

\noindent\textbf{Evaluation metrics.} CLEAR MOTA~\cite{clearmot} and
IDF1~\cite{idf1} weight detection and identity errors differently, while
HOTA~\cite{hota} factorises the two into DetA and AssA, which is what lets us
read detection and association quality separately over an \emph{identical} box
set. In 3D, nuScenes AMOTA~\cite{nuscenes_tracking,ab3dmot} is class-aware and
responds to labels the class-agnostic family ignores; video-consistency and
open-vocabulary tracking metrics~\cite{step,vpq,teta,tao,ovtrack} instead
require dense ground-truth tube identities over a fixed taxonomy, so we use
existing metrics where computable.

Existing work targets streaming availability or instance-level consistency,
but does not isolate temporal selection under matched output budgets, nor
measure the class-label stability of persistent instances.
